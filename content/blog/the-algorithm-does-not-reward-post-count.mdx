I was about to commit to posting four times a day on X. It felt like the obvious move: more
platform, more volume, more chances to be seen.

Before I touched the calendar, I checked what the 2026 ranking data actually says. It said
something different than what I assumed.

X moved its "For You" ranking to a Grok-powered model this year. The model reads a post,
works out what it actually means, and matches it to the people already talking about that
topic. It does not reward volume. It rewards engagement, and one kind especially: a reply
that gets a reply back from you is worth roughly 150 times a like, because it tells the
model someone is staying on the platform to keep talking, not just scrolling past.

Post count is the easy thing to control. So it is the thing almost everyone optimizes for,
four posts a day, ten posts a day, whatever number feels ambitious enough. But it is not the
thing the model actually pays attention to. Four posts a day that nobody replies to, and that
I never reply to either, lose to two posts I actually show up for in the comments.

I have seen this exact mistake before, just wearing different clothes. A procurement
operation adds a fifth tool, thinking more coverage means more control, when the real problem
was that the four tools it already had never talked to each other. Same shape here: a fourth
platform and a higher post count looked like progress, when the actual lever was replying to
the people already showing up, on the platform I am already on.

This is also not the first time I have caught myself about to copy a cadence I never actually
checked. Just last week it was doubling my LinkedIn posting on the assumption that more is
always better, until I found the source and it said the opposite. Same habit, same fix: check
the real number before you build a plan on top of it.

For now, X waits. LinkedIn is where the trust is being built right now, and I would rather
earn the right to add a platform than add one because it felt like progress.
